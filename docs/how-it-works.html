One run of the image model: it guesses the noise in a noisy picture and removes part of it.
Schedule position (timestep)
How noisy the picture is at a step, from 0 (most noise) to 49 (the least) in Dreamer's 50-position schedule.
UNet
The main network inside Stable Diffusion that does the guessing. It is named after its U shape: it shrinks the picture to see the big picture, then grows it back for detail.
ControlNet
A helper network that makes the UNet follow a guide image, here the child's lines.
Latent
A compressed version of the picture, 8× smaller on each side, where the model does its work. The VAE turns it back into pixels.
TensorRT engine
The model compiled by NVIDIA's TensorRT for this exact graphics card, so it runs much faster. Built once, on the first start.
Batch
Several pieces of work sent through the model together in one go. While drawing, Dreamer's 2 steps run as one batch.
Rung
One polishing step after the child pauses. The six rungs form a “ladder”, each using less noise than the last.
Low-pass
A filter that keeps only the broad, slowly changing parts of a picture (a blur). Dreamer uses it to pin the layout.
Drift
How much the broad layout changed compared to the first picture, on a 0–255 scale.

1 · The loop

From a finger on glass to a picture, and back

Everything happens on the home network. The iPad runs a plain web page saved to the home screen. With every stroke it sends what the child drew to one Python program on the PC, over a WebSocket (a connection that stays open, so each message goes straight through). The picture comes back on the same connection.

2 · What is a denoising step?

Pictures out of noise, one step at a time

Image models like the one in Dreamer are trained on a strange task: take a picture buried in random noise, and guess what the noise is. Remove part of that guess and the picture is a little clearer. Do it again and it is clearer still. Each of those guesses is a denoising step, and every step is one full run of the model on the graphics card.

Classic models need 20 to 50 steps. Dreamer uses SD-Turbo, a version of Stable Diffusion trained to take very big steps: its makers say 1 to 4 steps are enough. That is what makes a new picture per stroke possible.

Two more things the model gets

It does not start from pure noise.

Dreamer uses “img2img”: it starts from the colour-cloud image (stage 3) with noise mixed in. So the child's colours and the paper colour survive into the picture. Black paper gives a night scene, blue paper the deep sea.

It works on a small grid, not pixels.

The steps happen in a compressed “latent” grid, eight times smaller than the picture on each side. A small decoder (the Tiny VAE, stage 6) turns the result into real pixels at the end.

3 · Drawing fast, refining slowly

Keep up while the pen moves. Polish when it stops.

While the child draws, Dreamer runs 2 steps per picture, and runs them together as one batch through the model (StreamDiffusion's “denoising batch”). That keeps up with the pen, at the cost of each picture arriving one frame late, so once the child pauses Dreamer re-draws the last sketch once (“settle”).

When the child stops for 150 ms, the polishing starts: up to 6 single-step rungs, each 44–48 ms, each one replacing the picture on screen. A new stroke stops it after the rung in progress, and the pen is followed again straight away.

Why not simply keep going with more steps?

The obvious way to polish is to continue the model's own path with more steps. Dreamer's first version did exactly that, in passes of 4, 8, 12, 16 and 20 steps. By eye, the first few extra steps looked like the same picture getting sharper. Measured, the layout already shifts after the first extra step (drift 20–26 in the slider below), and from about 6 steps the picture visibly repaints the scene: in the close-ups, the cat's face is redrawn. SD-Turbo is made for 1 to 4 steps, not twenty.

Drag the slider to compare, on a real test drawing. Drift is how much the broad layout changed compared to the first picture (shrink both pictures to a tiny 40×28 thumbnail and measure the average difference, on a 0–255 scale). The target was to stay under about 6.

The fix: pin the broad shapes, let only the detail change

The anchored model treats every picture as two layers: the broad shapes (layout, big colour areas, light) and the fine detail on top. The polishing is a separate pass, not a continuation of the drawing's steps: each rung takes the picture now on screen, adds some noise back, and lets the model remove it in one step. In the model's answer, Dreamer then swaps the broad layer back to the one from the first picture, so mostly the detail can change. Technically it is a low-pass filter (keeps only the slow, broad variations) on the latent grid: a Gaussian blur of 2 latent pixels, about 16 picture pixels. The pin is full strength on the first rung and tapers to 80% on the last, so later rungs are slightly freer.

Two more tricks keep each change small. Each rung re-noises to a lower level than the one before (positions 38 → 41 → 44 → 46 → 47 → 48 on the schedule), so it can only touch finer things. And each rung builds on the previous one: the noise it adds back is mostly the model's own estimate of the noise, with only 5% of its variance fresh random noise. Detail builds up, and the scene mostly stays.

See it for yourself: blur the pictures so only the broad shapes remain. After 6 anchored rungs, the blurred picture stays close to the first one (a small shift remains, mostly from the first rung). The plain path moves much further.

Anchored has the lowest average max drift (5.6), almost the most detail, shrinking steps (9.0 → 0.9) and the fastest rungs (44 ms in the lab), so it is the default. It is not perfect: averaged it stays under 6, but the cat (8.3 at the first rung, 7.1 at the last) and the meadow (7.4–7.9) still go over, mostly at the first rung, and only 2 of 6 drawings pass the full rule (the others miss it by detail staying flat rather than rising). The tiled hi-res variants add slightly more detail but cost 175–295 ms per rung and drift more.

Optional: AI upscale

Polished pictures can also go through a small Real-ESRGAN model (realesr-animevideov3, 2.5 MB) that doubles them to 1280×896 in 31 ms. It is off by default, and never runs while the child is drawing.

4 · Prompts and safety

Children never type

Image models are steered with a text prompt. In Dreamer, nobody types one on the iPad. The child taps a topic and a style from the ones a parent allowed. The iPad only knows their names and emoji; the text itself lives on the PC, which builds the prompt in a fixed order and always ends with the same child-friendly phrase. Try it:

Topic
Style
fixed start topic (parent-approved) style (parent-approved) fixed child-friendly ending, always last

Parents manage the list from a PIN-protected page on their phone: hide topics, rename them, or write their own (up to 120 characters, for example “a pirate ship on a sunny sea”). The start phrase is deliberately “a colorful illustration” and not “a child's drawing”: the second one asked for a drawing look and kept the pen strokes.

A safety check on every picture

Before any picture is shown, an image safety classifier (Freepik/nsfw_image_detector, running as a TensorRT engine) looks at it. That costs about 4 ms per picture. If it flags a picture, the iPad simply keeps the previous one.

5 · Hardware and memory

Fitting it all in 10 GB

The models run as TensorRT engines: versions compiled for this exact graphics card, which is a big part of the speed. Building them takes roughly 10–25 minutes on the very first start; after that Dreamer is ready in about 8–10 seconds. Engines must fit in the card's memory together with the Windows desktop. By default Dreamer draws with 2 steps, using engines built for up to 4 steps at 640×448. The parent page estimates memory from a measured table and disables settings that would not fit.

Surprise from the measurements: engines for “up to 2 steps” need more memory than “up to 4” (measured twice). Picture size barely matters for memory, but a lot for speed.

6 · Why scribble ControlNet

Making the picture follow the drawing

On its own, the model only listens to the prompt. A ControlNet is a second network that runs next to it at every step and nudges it to follow a guide image: here, the line map from stage 2. Dreamer sets its strength to 0.55: enough for the shapes, not so much that it traces the pen strokes. At 0.35 the drawing got lost; at 0.8 the lines came back as thick outlines.

There are ControlNets trained on different kinds of lines. All were tested on the same six drawings:

The 7.6 GB was measured in a separate ControlNet lab process (marked “lab”), so it does not match the memory section exactly.

They all cost the same, so speed decides nothing. Lineart and HED are trained on thin edges of real images, so they read a child's thick strokes as outlines to reproduce, and draw the strokes instead of the thing. Scribble is trained on rough strokes and turns them into objects, which is exactly what a drawing game needs. Scribble stays. Smaller alternatives (ControlNet-XS, T2I-Adapter) have no sketch version for this model family, so there is no cheaper drop-in.

Private by design

Everything runs on the home network, in one program on the PC, with no account. Drawings and pictures never leave your home network. The models are downloaded once, on the first start; after that Dreamer switches its model loaders to offline mode and runs without contacting the internet. One exception: changing a model in config.yaml, or switching the safety filter on when it was off, may download once more.
